The World of Jellyfish

They have no brain, no heart, and no bones — yet jellyfish have drifted through the oceans for more than 500 million years. Built from water, nerves, and stinging cells, they include some of the most beautiful, and most venomous, animals on Earth.

01 · The numbers

How many jellyfish exist?

“Jellyfish” is a common name, not a single taxonomic group. It covers true jellyfish, box jellies, hydrozoans, and stalked jellies — but not comb jellies.

🪼

Living medusozoans · Medusozoa

Approx. 4,000+

Described living species of true jellyfish, box jellies, hydrozoans, and stalked jellies

The medusozoans — the subphylum that includes all “jellyfish” — break down into roughly 3,000–4,000 described Scyphozoa (true jellyfish), ~50 Cubozoa (box jellies), ~3,700 Hydrozoa (many of which are tiny polyps or colonial animals), and ~50 Staurozoa (stalked jellyfish). Comb jellies are not medusozoans — they belong to a separate phylum, Ctenophora, and only look similar.

Scyphozoa, approx. 3,000–4,000 species
Box jellies
Cubozoa, approx. 50 species

Counts are approximate and depend on the taxonomic source. The World Register of Marine Species (WoRMS) maintains accepted names for medusozoans and ctenophores.

02 · Meet the groups

Five animals people call “jellyfish”

Some are true jellyfish. Others are close relatives, and one isn’t a jellyfish at all — it’s a separate phylum that happens to look similar.

🌕
ScyphozoaTrue jellyfish

Moon jelly

Aurelia aurita

Size: Bell usually 25–40 cm across.

Habitat: Coastal waters worldwide; often in harbours, bays, and estuaries.

Fact: The four horseshoe-shaped rings visible through its clear bell are gonads. Moon jellies tolerate low oxygen and can form large seasonal blooms.

☠️
CubozoaMost venomous

Australian box jellyfish

Chironex fleckeri

Size: Bell around 20 cm; tentacles up to 3 m.

Habitat: Tropical coastal waters of northern Australia and the Indo-Pacific.

Fact: Considered the most venomous marine animal known. Its stings can kill a human within minutes, but most encounters are avoidable with protective suits.

👑
ScyphozoaLongest tentacles

Lion’s mane jellyfish

Cyanea capillata

Size: Bell up to about 2 m; tentacles reported to reach 36.5 m.

Habitat: Cold North Atlantic, North Pacific, and Arctic waters.

Fact: One of the largest known jellyfish. It has been recorded with the longest tentacles of any animal, though such extreme specimens are exceptional.

🌈
HydrozoaColonial

Portuguese man o’ war

Physalia physalis

Size: Float around 10–30 cm; tentacles up to 30 m.

Habitat: Warm surface waters of the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific oceans.

Fact: Not a single animal, but a colonial siphonophore made of specialised individuals working together. Its stings remain painful long after the animal washes ashore.

💡
CtenophoraNot a jellyfish

Sea gooseberry

Pleurobrachia pileus

Size: Body around 2–3 cm; tentacles much longer.

Habitat: Coastal and open waters of the North Atlantic and North Pacific.

Fact: A comb jelly — not a cnidarian. Its “combs” of beating cilia scatter light into rainbow bands. It captures prey with sticky tentacles rather than stinging cells.

“Jellyfish” is a common name. The word covers several medusozoan classes, and it is sometimes stretched to include comb jellies — which are not closely related.

Scene 01 · The Bloom

A cluster of jellyfish pulses together, drifting gently through the water

A bloom can form when currents, food, or temperature bring many jellyfish together. Here, seven pulse at staggered phases — each contraction pushes a little water downward, lifting the animal slightly upward — while their tentacles trail and sway.

03 · The deep sea

Jellyfish of the deep

Below the sunlit zone, jellyfish take on unfamiliar forms — many glow, some are enormous, and a few look more like abstract art than animals.

DEEP · 01

Atolla wyvillei

Crown jellyfish

  • Depth: approx. 500–5,000 m
  • Size: bell up to about 15 cm
  • Range: all oceans

When attacked, this dark red jellyfish produces a spectacular ring of blue flashes — a “burglar alarm” that may attract a larger predator to scare off its attacker.

DEEP · 02

Deepstaria enigmatica

Giant deep-sea jelly

  • Depth: approx. 600–1,750 m
  • Size: bell can reach about 1 m across
  • Range: widely scattered, all oceans

Its bell looks like a thin, translucent sheet that opens and closes like an umbrella. It has no tentacles, and its feeding behaviour is still poorly known.

DEEP · 03

Crossota millsae

A red deep-sea jelly

  • Depth: approx. 1,000–3,700 m
  • Size: bell around 1.5–3 cm
  • Range: Pacific and Atlantic

Small but striking: its red body with trailing tentacles is often filmed by deep-diving submersibles. Females brood their young inside the bell — a form of parental care unusual in jellyfish.

DEEP · 04

Halitrephes maasi

“Fireworks jellyfish”

  • Depth: approx. 1,200–1,500 m
  • Size: bell around 10 cm
  • Range: deep waters of the Pacific

Its radial canals glow bright pink and yellow when illuminated by a submersible’s lights, giving the appearance of a firework frozen mid-burst. Its natural bioluminescence is not yet well studied.

Depth records differ between sources and are based on relatively few observations. Values above are approximate reference ranges.

04 · Regional focus

Jellyfish of Asian and European waters

The Indo-Pacific and the Northeast Atlantic hold very different jellyfish communities, from giant blooms to tiny venomous box jellies.

ASIA · 01

Nomura’s jellyfish

Nemopilema nomurai

  • Depth: mostly surface to a few hundred metres
  • Size: bell up to about 2 m; weight up to 200 kg
  • Range: East China Sea, Yellow Sea, Sea of Japan

One of the largest jellyfish in the world. Its periodic blooms clog fishing nets and have caused significant damage to Japanese fisheries.

ASIA · 02

Spotted lagoon jelly

Mastigias papua

  • Depth: shallow lagoons and lakes
  • Size: bell around 10–15 cm
  • Range: Indo-Pacific; famous in Palau’s Jellyfish Lake

Symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) live inside its tissues and supply it with energy from photosynthesis. In Jellyfish Lake, huge daytime migrations follow the sun.

EUROPE · 01

Moon jelly

Aurelia aurita

  • Depth: shallow coastal waters
  • Size: bell usually 25–40 cm
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic, Mediterranean, and other temperate coasts

A familiar jellyfish of European harbours and bays. Blooms form in spring and summer, and they are harmless to most people — their stings are weak and rarely felt.

EUROPE · 02

Compass jellyfish

Chrysaora hysoscella

  • Depth: shallow coastal waters
  • Size: bell around 15–30 cm
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic and Mediterranean

Named for the brown V-shaped markings on its bell, which resemble the points of a compass. Its sting is painful but usually not dangerous.

EUROPE · 03

Lion’s mane jellyfish

Cyanea capillata

  • Depth: surface to roughly 20 m, following currents
  • Size: bell up to 2 m; tentacles to 36 m in extreme records
  • Range: North Atlantic, North Sea, and Arctic

Large specimens are more often reported in colder northern waters. Its tentacles can deliver painful stings and are still active after the animal has washed ashore.

Regional depth and size ranges are compiled from species databases, regional assessments, and survey records.

Scene 02 · The Glow

A single bioluminescent jellyfish pulses in dark water, surrounded by orbiting light particles

In the deep sea, darkness is the rule — and light is a weapon, a lure, or a warning. Many jellyfish produce their own glow (bioluminescence), sometimes as a defence: bright flashes can startle a predator or attract a larger animal that might eat it.

05 · Life cycle

How jellyfish reproduce

Most jellyfish alternate between a free-swimming medusa and a bottom-dwelling polyp. Their life cycle can include several dramatic transformations.

01 · MEDUSA

The adult form

The bell-shaped animal most people recognise. Adults release eggs and sperm into the water, where fertilisation occurs.

02 · PLANULA

A swimming larva

The fertilised egg becomes a small, ciliated planula larva that swims for a few days before settling onto the seafloor.

03 · POLYP

Anchored to rock

The planula settles and grows into a polyp — a small, stalked animal anchored to rock or shell. It can live for years and can bud off new polyps asexually.

04 · STROBILA

A stack of discs

In a process called strobilation, the polyp divides into stacked discs. Each disc will become a juvenile jellyfish.

05 · EPHYRA

Tiny free-swimmers

One by one, the discs break off as ephyrae — tiny, star-shaped juveniles that swim away and gradually grow into adults.

VARIATION

Not every jellyfish follows this script

Box jellies skip the polyp stage in some species. A few, like Turritopsis dohrnii, can reverse the cycle — reverting from medusa back to polyp when stressed.

The polyp → strobila → ephyra → medusa sequence describes many Scyphozoa. Hydrozoans and cubozoans differ, and a few species reproduce only asexually.

06 · Food webs

What do jellyfish eat?

Jellyfish are almost all carnivorous, capturing tiny prey with stinging cells on their tentacles and passing it to a central mouth.

PLANKTON

A diet of drifters

Many jellyfish feed on plankton — copepods, tiny crustaceans, fish eggs, and larvae. Some passively strain water as they pulse; others actively swim to capture prey.

FISH & INVERTEBRATES

Bigger meals

Larger jellies take small fish, shrimp, and other jellyfish. Lion’s mane jellyfish are known to feed on moon jellies and even on other lion’s manes.

ALGAL PARTNERS

A photosynthetic supplement

Some shallow-water species, like the spotted lagoon jelly, host symbiotic zooxanthellae. The algae photosynthesise during the day, supplying the jellyfish with extra energy.

DEEP-SEA SCAVENGERS

A meal from the deep

Some deep-sea jellyfish, including Deepstaria, appear to feed on whatever drifts past — detritus, small invertebrates, and particles settling from above.

07 · Adaptations

Stings, glows, and other tricks

Jellyfish don’t have brains — but their cells are specialists, each performing a task that keeps the animal alive.

NEMATOCYSTS

Stinging cells

Each tentacle carries thousands of stinging cells, called nematocysts. When triggered, they fire a tiny harpoon loaded with venom — one of the fastest cellular processes in biology.

BIOLUMINESCENCE

Making their own light

Many jellyfish produce light via a chemical reaction involving the protein luciferin. Some use it to startle predators; others to attract prey or a mate.

JET PROPULSION

Squeeze and glide

By contracting the bell, a jellyfish forces water out and produces a small jet of thrust. Between contractions, it glides with its tentacles trailing.

STATOCYSTS

Knowing which way is up

Box jellies have balance organs containing tiny crystals that tell the animal which way is down — essential for a swimmer that needs to stay oriented.

SYMBIOSIS

Partners in the sun

Some jellyfish host algae, small fish, or crustaceans that shelter under their bell or among their tentacles. In return, the jellyfish gets nutrients or cleaning services.

BLOOMS

When populations explode

Blooms can follow overfishing (which removes jellyfish predators), nutrient runoff, or warming waters. Bloom events increasingly interfere with fisheries and coastal industry.

08 · Did you know?

Five facts to take to the surface

Jellyfish are older than trees, stranger than their simple look suggests, and more important to the ocean than their gelatinous appearance implies.

FACT · 01

Not fish at all

Jellyfish are cnidarians — the same phylum as corals and sea anemones. They have no brain, no heart, no bones, and are roughly 95 % water.

FACT · 02

Older than trees

Jellyfish appear in the fossil record more than 500 million years ago — long before the first fish, and hundreds of millions of years before trees.

FACT · 03

The immortal jellyfish

Turritopsis dohrnii can revert from its adult stage back to a juvenile polyp when stressed. In principle, this loop could continue indefinitely, which is why it earned its nickname.

FACT · 04

Eyes without a brain

Box jellies have up to 24 eyes — including some with lenses — arranged in clusters on their bell. They use them to navigate toward light and avoid obstacles.

FACT · 05

A burglar alarm in the dark

When attacked, the deep-sea crown jelly Atolla wyvillei flashes a ring of blue light. The display may attract a larger predator, which attacks the jelly’s attacker — a behaviour known as a “burglar alarm.”

09 · A changing ocean

Jellyfish in a shifting sea

Jellyfish populations are hard to assess — most species are not on the IUCN Red List. But patterns of blooms and declines tell a story about ecosystem health.

Jellyfish are not a conservation priority in the way whales or sharks are — but their populations are sensitive to human activity. Overfishing of jellyfish predators, warming seas, nutrient runoff, and coastal construction have all been linked to large blooms in some regions. Meanwhile, a few populations — such as those in Palau’s Jellyfish Lake — have crashed dramatically. Some researchers describe jellyfish as winners in a changing ocean; others warn that the “rise of slime” narrative oversimplifies a complex picture. Either way, tracking jellyfish is one way to read the health of the sea.
