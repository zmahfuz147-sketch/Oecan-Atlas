The World of Sharks & Rays

Built from cartilage, tuned to electricity, and armed with teeth that never stop renewing, sharks and rays have patrolled the oceans for more than 400 million years. They are among the most successful—and most misunderstood—vertebrates ever to swim.

01 · The numbers

How many sharks and rays exist?

Sharks, rays, and skates together make up the Elasmobranchii—the cartilaginous fishes. Their exact count shifts as new species are described.

🦈

Living elasmobranchs · Elasmobranchii

Approx. 1,200+

Described living sharks, rays, and skates combined

Modern checklists recognise roughly 500 to 600 living shark species (Selachii) and more than 600 living rays and skates (Batoidea). Their skeletons are made of flexible cartilage rather than bone, and their skin is covered in tiny tooth-like scales called dermal denticles. Rays are, in evolutionary terms, flattened sharks—their gill slits sit on the underside of the body, and their pectoral fins form broad “wings.”

Sharks
Approx. 500–600 species (Selachii)
Rays & skates
Approx. 600+ species (Batoidea)
Why it changes
New descriptions and molecular revisions

Counts are approximate and depend on the taxonomic source and its update date. The World Register of Marine Species (WoRMS) and the IUCN Red List maintain accepted names and assessments for many elasmobranch groups.

02 · Meet the groups

Five sharks and rays worth knowing

From a plankton-eating giant to a venomous river ray, elasmobranchs span an extraordinary range of shapes and hunting styles.

🌊
LamniformesApex predator

Great white shark

Carcharodon carcharias

Size: Typically 4–5.5 m; the largest reliably measured around 6 m.

Habitat: Temperate and subtropical coastal waters, offshore islands, and open-ocean crossings.

Fact: Like some other lamnids, it is partly warm-blooded, retaining metabolic heat to stay active in cool water.

👑
OrectolobiformesLargest fish

Whale shark

Rhincodon typus

Size: Commonly 10–12 m; the largest confirmed individuals reach roughly 18 m.

Habitat: Warm surface waters of the tropical and subtropical Indo-Pacific and Atlantic.

Fact: A filter feeder that strains plankton through gill rakers—the largest fish eats some of the smallest prey.

🔨
CarcharhiniformesSpecialised head

Great hammerhead

Sphyrna mokarran

Size: Up to roughly 6 m—the largest hammerhead.

Habitat: Warm coastal waters, reefs, and offshore shelves worldwide.

Fact: Its wide, flattened head carries widely spaced electroreceptors and nostrils, which may help it sweep the seafloor for hidden prey.

🛸
MyliobatiformesGentle giant

Giant oceanic manta ray

Mobula birostris

Size: Wing span commonly 4–5 m; the largest reach about 7 m.

Habitat: Open tropical and subtropical seas, often near seamounts and cleaning stations.

Fact: A filter feeder with forward-pointing cephalic fins that funnel plankton into its mouth. Each ray’s belly pattern is unique, like a fingerprint.

⚡
MyliobatiformesVenomous

Blue-spotted ribbontail ray

Taeniura lymma

Size: Disc width typically around 25–35 cm.

Habitat: Shallow coral reefs and sandy patches in the Indo-Pacific.

Fact: Bright blue spots warn of venomous tail spines. It buries itself in sand by day and hunts molluscs and crustaceans at night.

“Shark” and “ray” are common names, not strict taxonomic ranks. Rays evolved from shark-like ancestors, and some fossil sharks look much more like rays than like modern sharks.

03 · The deep sea

Sharks and rays of the deep

Below the sunlit zone, sharks slow down, grow slowly, and sometimes glow. Some of these animals are among the least-known vertebrates on Earth.

DEEP · 01

Goblin shark

Mitsukurina owstoni

  • Depth: mostly below 200 m; records past 1,300 m
  • Size: usually 3–4 m
  • Range: scattered records worldwide

Pale pink skin, a long flattened snout, and jaws that fire forward define this lamniform. Its jaws can shoot out almost the length of its head to snap up prey.

DEEP · 02

Frilled shark

Chlamydoselachus anguineus

  • Depth: typically 500–1,000 m; recorded deeper
  • Size: up to about 2 m
  • Range: Atlantic and Pacific slopes

With an eel-like body and tricuspid teeth in rows, the frilled shark hunts squid and fish in the twilight zone. Its gill slits form a frilled collar behind the head.

DEEP · 03

Portuguese dogfish

Centroscymnus coelolepis

  • Depth: approx. 300–3,600 m
  • Size: up to about 1.2 m
  • Range: Atlantic and western Pacific slopes

A stout, dark sleeper shark that lives far below sunlight. Deep-living sharks like this grow slowly, mature late, and produce few young—making them especially vulnerable.

DEEP · 04

Pacific white skate

Bathyraja spinosissima

  • Depth: records to below 2,900 m
  • Size: up to about 1.5 m across
  • Range: eastern Pacific, especially the Galápagos region

One of the deepest-living rays known. It lays large egg cases—researchers have found them clustered near hydrothermal vents, where warm water may speed development.

Depth records and maximum sizes differ between databases, regions, and survey methods. Values above are approximate reference ranges.

05 · Regional focus

Sharks and rays of Asian and European waters

The Indo-Pacific and the Northeast Atlantic hold very different elasmobranch communities, from reef rays to cold-water sleeper sharks.

ASIA · 01

Whale shark

Rhincodon typus

  • Depth: mostly surface to a few hundred metres
  • Size: up to roughly 18 m
  • Range: Indo-Pacific, including Japan, Taiwan, and the Philippines

Whale sharks gather at predictable coastal feeding sites in the Indo-Pacific, where plankton blooms draw them near the surface. Each shark carries a unique pattern of spots used by researchers to identify individuals.

ASIA · 02

Japanese bullhead shark

Heterodontus japonicus

  • Depth: shallow coastal waters, roughly 6–40 m
  • Size: up to about 1.2 m
  • Range: northwestern Pacific, around Japan and Korea

A small, nocturnal bottom-dweller with blunt molar-like teeth for crushing shells. Females lay distinctive spiral egg cases that they wedge into rock crevices.

EUROPE · 01

Basking shark

Cetorhinus maximus

  • Depth: surface to a few hundred metres, following plankton
  • Size: commonly 6–8 m; reports near 10 m
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic, including British and Irish coasts

A gentle filter feeder that cruises with its mouth open, straining plankton through enormous gill rakers. It is assessed as endangered in the Northeast Atlantic.

EUROPE · 02

Thornback ray

Raja clavata

  • Depth: roughly 10–300 m
  • Size: disc width up to about 1 m
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic and Mediterranean

A common skate-like ray with thorny dorsal patches. It feeds on crustaceans and small fish and lays rectangular egg cases—the familiar “mermaid’s purses.”

EUROPE · 03

Lesser spotted catshark

Scyliorhinus canicula

  • Depth: roughly 10–400 m
  • Size: up to about 1 m
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic and Mediterranean

One of the most abundant sharks of European shelves. Females attach rectangular egg cases to algae and rocks, and embryos develop for several months before hatching.

Regional depth and size ranges are compiled from species databases, regional assessments, and survey records. Local populations may fall outside these ranges.

06 · Deep time

Famous sharks and rays from the fossil record

Because elasmobranch skeletons are cartilage, the fossil record is dominated by teeth and fin spines. Even so, it preserves some of the most spectacular predators in Earth’s history.

FOSSIL · 01

Otodus megalodon

The largest shark known

  • Time: approx. 23–3.6 million years ago
  • Size: most estimates fall around 15–18 m
  • Known from: teeth and rare vertebral remains worldwide

Fossils only. Its hand-sized teeth suggest a predator capable of hunting large whales. No complete skeleton exists, so body shape and maximum length remain active research questions.

FOSSIL · 02

Cladoselache

An early shark-like chondrichthyan

  • Time: approx. 380 million years ago, Devonian
  • Size: roughly 1–2 m
  • Known from: exceptionally preserved fossils in Ohio, USA

Fossils only. Some specimens preserve traces of muscle and fins, giving a rare glimpse of early chondrichthyan anatomy. It had a streamlined body but lacked tooth-like scales over much of its skin.

FOSSIL · 03

Heliobatis radians

An early freshwater stingray

  • Time: approx. 52 million years ago, Eocene
  • Size: disc width of roughly 30 cm
  • Known from: Fossil Lake, Wyoming, USA

Fossils only. Complete specimens from the Green River Formation preserve the flattened disc, tail, and even stomach contents—showing that this ancient ray fed on small fish and invertebrates.

Thousands of fossil elasmobranch species have been described, most of them from isolated teeth. Reconstructions of extinct species are continually revised.

07 · Life cycle

How sharks and rays reproduce

Elasmobranchs invest heavily in relatively few, large young. Their strategies range from egg-laying to live birth—and include some remarkable extremes.

01 · MATING

Claspers and courtship

Males transfer sperm using a pair of modified pelvic fins called claspers. Mating often involves the male gripping the female’s pectoral fin, leaving visible bite marks in some species.

02 · OVIPARITY

Egg cases

Many catsharks, bullhead sharks, and skates lay eggs encased in tough capsules—often called mermaid’s purses. The capsules may be anchored to rocks, algae, or the seafloor.

03 · LIVE BIRTH

Pups, not larvae

Most sharks and rays are live-bearing. Embryos may feed on yolk, on secretions from the mother’s uterus, or on unfertilised eggs. Pups emerge as miniature adults.

04 · INTRAUTERINE CANNIBALISM

The biggest embryo wins

In sand tiger sharks, the first embryo to develop teeth eats its siblings inside the uterus. As a result, only one or two pups are born from each litter—already experienced predators.

05 · SLOW GROWTH

Late to mature

Many elasmobranchs take a decade or more to reach maturity and produce few young per breeding cycle. This makes populations slow to recover when adults are removed by fishing.

VARIATION

Not every species is the same

Litter size, gestation length, and breeding frequency differ enormously. Deep-sea species in particular often grow and reproduce more slowly than their shallow-water relatives.

The strategies described here cover common patterns; many species deviate in gestation, litter size, and nursery habitat.

08 · Food webs

What do sharks and rays eat?

There is no single elasmobranch diet. Some are apex predators, some strain plankton, and some crush shells on the seafloor.

APEX PREDATORS

Fish, seals, and larger prey

Great whites, makos, and other large lamnids hunt fish, squid, and marine mammals. Their role in shaping prey behaviour can ripple through entire ecosystems.

FILTER FEEDERS

A diet of plankton

Whale sharks, basking sharks, and manta rays strain tiny plankton and small fish from enormous volumes of water. They are among the largest animals to feed on the smallest prey.

CRUSHERS

Shellfish specialists

Heterodontid bullhead sharks and many rays use flat, molar-like teeth to crush molluscs, crustaceans, and echinoderms. Their slow, deliberate hunting suits bottom habitats.

SCAVENGERS

A meal from the seafloor

Greenland sharks and some deep-water species take carrion, including large whale carcasses. Scavenging helps recycle nutrients in food-poor deep environments.

09 · Adaptations

Senses and special abilities

Sharks and rays detect electricity, smell at extraordinary distances, and feel the water move around them—senses tuned to a world of low light and long distances.

ELECTRORECEPTION

Ampullae of Lorenzini

Pits around the snout detect weak electric fields produced by the muscles of hidden prey. Hammerheads’ wide heads spread these receptors across a broad area, aiding bottom-hunting.

LATERAL LINE

Feeling the water

A canal system along the body senses vibration and water movement, letting a shark detect a struggling fish or an approaching predator even in darkness.

SKIN

Dermal denticles

Elasmobranch skin is covered in tiny tooth-like scales that reduce drag and discourage parasites. Their ribbed surface has even inspired designs for swimming suits and ship hulls.

TEETH

A conveyor belt of teeth

Sharks grow rows of replacement teeth that move forward as older ones are lost. A single shark may use hundreds or thousands of teeth in a lifetime.

SIXTH SENSE

Reading the field

Some sharks and rays may use the earth’s magnetic field to navigate long distances. Researchers are still working out how the sensory system detects it.

MIGRATION

Ocean-spanning journeys

Satellite tracking shows that some great whites, makos, whale sharks, and manta rays cross entire ocean basins. Their routes often follow productive fronts and seasonal prey movements.

10 · Did you know?

Five facts to take to the surface

Sharks and rays are far older, stranger, and more varied than their fearsome reputation suggests.

FACT · 01

Older than trees

Shark-like fishes appear in the fossil record more than 400 million years ago—before trees, and long before the first dinosaurs.

FACT · 02

Skeletons without bone

An elasmobranch skeleton is made of cartilage, which is lighter and more flexible than bone. Their jaws are the main exception, reinforced with calcium salts.

FACT · 03

Rays are flattened sharks

In evolutionary terms, rays are sharks with flattened bodies and enlarged pectoral fins. Their gill slits lie on the underside of the body rather than the sides.

FACT · 04

Danger, in perspective

Unprovoked shark bites cause a handful of fatalities worldwide each year, while estimates of sharks and rays killed by fishing reach tens of millions annually.

FACT · 05

Manta fingerprints

Every manta ray has a unique pattern of spots on its belly. Researchers photograph these patterns to identify individuals—just like fingerprints in a human database.

11 · A changing ocean

Sharks and rays under pressure

Slow growth and low reproduction make many elasmobranchs vulnerable. Science-based limits, protected areas, and bycatch reduction are central to their future.

A large share of assessed shark and ray species are threatened with extinction. Overfishing—both targeted and as bycatch—is the leading pressure, compounded by habitat loss, pollution, and climate change. Because many species mature late and produce few young, populations recover slowly once depleted. Conservation tools include science-based catch limits, finning bans, shark sanctuaries, gear modifications to reduce bycatch, and international trade regulation for heavily exploited species.

Main threat
Overfishing and bycatch
Why recovery is slow
Late maturity, few young, long lives
Key tools
Catch limits, protected areas, bycatch reduction

Conservation status differs widely between species and regions. Always check the most recent IUCN Red List assessment for a particular species and population.
