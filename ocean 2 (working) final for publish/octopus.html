The World of Octopuses

With three hearts, blue blood, and arms that taste what they touch, octopuses are among the most unusual animals in the sea. From shallow reefs to abyssal plains deeper than 7,000 metres, these soft-bodied cephalopods have evolved extraordinary solutions for survival.

  • 🌊 From tide pools to abyssal depths
  • 🧠 Advanced problem-solving and camouflage

01 · The numbers

How many octopuses exist?

Octopoda is a diverse order, but its species count is still being refined as new animals are described and genetics reshape old groupings.

🐙

Living octopuses · Octopoda

Approx. 300+

Described living species of octopus

The order Octopoda contains roughly 300 recognised living species, divided into two main suborders: Cirrata (finned, deep-sea octopuses with small internal shells) and Incirrata (the more familiar shell-less octopuses of shallow and deep waters). Some estimates place the total higher, and “catch-all” genera such as Octopus are being split as genetic tools improve.

02 · Meet the groups

Five octopuses worth knowing

From the giant Pacific octopus to the tiny blue-ringed octopus, the group spans a spectacular range of sizes, habitats, and survival strategies.

🐙
IncirrataFamiliar octopus

Common octopus

Octopus vulgaris

Size: Typically around 60–100 cm total length, though arm span varies.

Habitat: Coastal reefs, seagrass beds, and rocky bottoms in temperate and tropical waters.

Fact: A highly flexible predator and one of the most studied octopuses for problem-solving and camouflage.

👑
IncirrataGiant

Giant Pacific octopus

Enteroctopus dofleini

Size: The world’s largest octopus; arm span can reach 9 m or more.

Habitat: North Pacific coasts and slopes, from the intertidal to over 1,000 m.

Fact: Females brood their eggs for months without feeding and die shortly after they hatch.

🐘
CirrataDeep sea

Dumbo octopus

Genus Grimpoteuthis

Size: Usually small, roughly 20–30 cm, depending on species.

Habitat: Deep seafloor worldwide, recorded from about 100 m to over 7,000 m.

Fact: It swims with ear-like fins rather than jet propulsion and lacks an ink sac.

💙
IncirrataVenomous

Blue-ringed octopus

Genus Hapalochlaena

Size: Small, with an arm spread of about 10–20 cm.

Habitat: Tide pools and shallow reefs in the Indo-Pacific.

Fact: Its bite delivers tetrodotoxin, a neurotoxin far more potent than cyanide; the rings flash when threatened.

🎭
IncirrataMimic

Mimic octopus

Thaumoctopus mimicus

Size: Relatively small, with arms around 60 cm long.

Habitat: Sandy and muddy bottoms in the Indo-Pacific.

Fact: It can impersonate sea snakes, lionfish, and other animals by changing posture and colour.

“Octopus” is an informal name for animals in the order Octopoda. Not every eight-armed cephalopod is closely related, and some groups—such as argonauts—are only distant relatives.

03 · The deep sea

Octopuses of the deep

Below the sunlit zone, octopuses take on unfamiliar forms: finned, ghostly, and adapted to a world of darkness, cold, and scarce food.

DEEP · 01

Dumbo octopus

Grimpoteuthis spp.

  • Depth: approx. 100–7,000 m
  • Size: small, often 20–30 cm
  • Suborder: Cirrata

These cirrate octopuses use large fins to swim just above the seafloor. They have webbed arms, lack an ink sac, and are among the deepest-living octopuses known.

DEEP · 02

Graneledone boreopacifica

A deep-water incirrate octopus

  • Depth: mostly deeper than 650 m
  • Size: medium-bodied, with a single row of suckers on each arm
  • Range: North Pacific, including the Bering Sea

This species is notable for brooding its eggs for an exceptionally long time—one observed female guarded her clutch for about 4.5 years, the longest known brooding period for any animal.

DEEP · 03

Flapjack octopus

Opisthoteuthis spp.

  • Depth: approx. 300–1,100 m
  • Shape: flattened, umbrella-like
  • Suborder: Cirrata

With its rounded body and webbed arms, the flapjack octopus looks almost like a pancake with fins. It hovers above soft bottoms and is occasionally observed by deep-diving remotely operated vehicles.

Depth ranges are approximate and based on records compiled in species databases and deep-sea surveys. Many deep-sea octopuses are known from only a few sightings.

04 · Regional focus

Octopuses of Asian and European waters

From the North Pacific giant to the horned octopus of the Northeast Atlantic, regional species reveal different solutions to life on the seafloor.

ASIA · 01

Giant Pacific octopus

Enteroctopus dofleini

  • Depth: intertidal to approx. 1,500 m, depending on region
  • Size: arm span up to approx. 9 m
  • Range: North Pacific, including Japan and the Sea of Okhotsk

In Japan, this species supports important coastal fisheries and shows seasonal depth movements. It is the largest octopus in the world by mass and arm span.

EUROPE · 01

Horned octopus

Eledone cirrhosa

  • Depth: approx. 100–700 m, typically 250–500 m
  • Size: moderate, with a mantle length around 10–15 cm
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic and Mediterranean

Also called the curled octopus, it is a common benthic predator on continental shelves and slopes, feeding on crustaceans and fish.

EUROPE · 02

Arctic deep-sea octopus

Bathypolypus arcticus

  • Depth: recorded to approx. 1,543 m
  • Size: small to medium
  • Range: North Atlantic and Arctic waters

A cold-water species adapted to deep, soft-bottom habitats. Its biology is still poorly known, but it is regularly caught in deep-sea surveys.

Regional depth and size ranges are compiled from ICES, FAO, and species database records. Local populations may differ.

05 · Deep time

Fossil octopuses and their relatives

Because octopuses are almost entirely soft-bodied, their fossils are rare and often controversial. A handful of sites preserve enough detail to hint at their ancient history.

FOSSIL · 01

Pohlsepia mazonensis

Late Carboniferous, approx. 296 Ma

  • Location: Mazon Creek, Illinois, USA
  • Age: approx. 296 million years
  • Status: debated

Originally described as the oldest octopus, Pohlsepia shows a sac-like body and possible arm impressions. Later studies have questioned whether it truly belongs to Octopoda, making its position uncertain.

FOSSIL · 02

Proteroctopus ribeti

Middle Jurassic, approx. 165 Ma

  • Location: La Voulte-sur-Rhône, France
  • Age: Middle Jurassic
  • Status: possible early octopod

This fossil preserves a body outline with eight arms. Its classification has shifted between octopod and more basal octobrachian relatives, reflecting how difficult it is to place soft-bodied fossils with confidence.

FOSSIL · 03

Keuppia and Styletoctopus

Late Cretaceous, approx. 95 Ma

  • Location: Lebanon and Mexico
  • Age: Cenomanian–Turonian
  • Status: early octopods

These Cretaceous fossils are among the oldest relatively complete octopod body fossils. They preserve traces of the internal shell (gladius) and help calibrate when octopuses diverged from their relatives.

The octopus fossil record is sparse. Many alleged early octopuses are known from single specimens, and their evolutionary positions are actively debated.

06 · Life cycle

How octopuses reproduce

Most octopuses are semelparous: they breed once and die. Their reproductive strategies range from brief pelagic matings to years-long egg brooding.

01 · HECTOCOTYLUS

A specialised arm

Males transfer sperm using a modified arm called the hectocotylus. In many species, the male inserts the arm into the female’s mantle cavity, where spermatophores are stored until fertilisation.

02 · EGGS

A guarded clutch

Females attach fertilised eggs to hard surfaces in dens or crevices. Many species guard and aerate the eggs, often for months, without feeding.

03 · PARALARVAE

Tiny planktonic young

Hatchlings of many shallow-water octopuses are paralarvae: small, transparent, and planktonic for weeks to months. They drift with currents before settling to the bottom.

04 · SEMELPARITY

Breed once, then die

Both males and females typically die after spawning. Females invest heavily in egg care, and the post-spawning decline is linked to hormonal changes and starvation.

05 · DEEP-SEA BROODING

Years of patience

In cold, deep water, development is slow. Graneledone boreopacifica has been observed brooding eggs for about 4.5 years—the longest known brooding period of any animal.

VARIATION

Not every octopus is the same

Some deep-sea cirrates produce large, yolky eggs and may have extended or continuous spawning. Reproductive patterns vary widely across Octopoda.

The hectocotylus–egg–paralarya sequence describes many common octopuses, but deep-sea and cirrate species often deviate from this pattern.

07 · Food webs

What do octopuses eat?

Octopuses are versatile predators and scavengers, using their arms, suckers, and beak to capture and dismantle prey.

PREDATORS

Crustaceans and molluscs

Many octopuses specialise in crabs, lobsters, and bivalves. They use their beak to crack shells and their arms to pull prey apart.

FISH

An active hunter

Larger species, including the giant Pacific octopus, take fish and other cephalopods. They may ambush prey from a den or stalk it across the seafloor.

SCAVENGERS

Opportunistic feeders

Deep-sea octopuses will eat carrion and detritus when available. Scavenging is especially important in food-poor abyssal environments.

SPECIALISTS

A varied menu

Some species feed heavily on small crustaceans or worms; others are generalists. Diet often reflects local prey availability and the octopus’s size.

08 · Adaptations

Behaviour and remarkable abilities

Octopuses are masters of disguise, escape, and problem-solving. Their intelligence is among the most complex of any invertebrate.

CAMOUFLAGE

Masters of disguise

Using chromatophores, papillae, and posture, octopuses can change colour, pattern, and texture in milliseconds. Some species mimic specific animals or objects.

TOOL USE

Coconut-shell shelter

The veined octopus (Amphioctopus marginatus) collects discarded coconut shells and uses them as portable shelters—a rare example of tool use in invertebrates.

ESCAPE

Ink and jet propulsion

When threatened, many shallow-water octopuses release ink and shoot away using a powerful jet of water through their siphon. Deep-sea cirrates often lack an ink sac.

VENOM

A tiny but potent bite

Blue-ringed octopuses inject tetrodotoxin, a neurotoxin that can paralyse prey and deter predators. Their bright blue rings are a warning display.

09 · Did you know?

Five facts about octopuses

Their biology is as strange as their appearance—from three hearts to RNA editing.

FACT · 01

Three hearts, blue blood

Two branchial hearts pump blood through the gills, while a systemic heart serves the rest of the body. Their blood is blue because it uses haemocyanin, a copper-based oxygen carrier.

FACT · 02

Taste by touch

Suckers on the arms contain chemoreceptors, allowing octopuses to “taste” surfaces they touch. This helps them identify prey and explore crevices.

FACT · 03

RNA editing

Octopuses edit their RNA extensively, especially in nerve cells. This may help them adapt to temperature changes and generate protein diversity beyond what their DNA encodes.

FACT · 04

A huge nervous system

About two-thirds of an octopus’s neurons are distributed in its arms, not its central brain. Each arm can act semi-autonomously while exploring.

FACT · 05

Short lives, intense lives

Many shallow-water octopuses live only one to two years. They grow rapidly, reproduce once, and die—packing remarkable learning and behaviour into a brief lifespan.
