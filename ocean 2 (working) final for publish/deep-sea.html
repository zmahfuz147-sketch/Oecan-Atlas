The Deep Sea

Below 200 metres, sunlight fades to nothing. What remains is the largest habitat on Earth — a world of crushing pressure, near-freezing water, and animals that make their own light. Meet the anglerfish, giant squid, vampire squid, gulper eel, and the other extraordinary creatures of the abyss.

01 · The unknown majority

How many deep-sea species exist?

No one knows. The deep sea is the largest habitat on the planet, and most of its animals have never been described.

🌑

The largest habitat on Earth

Unknown — but vast

Hundreds of thousands to over 10 million estimated species, most undescribed

The deep sea — everything below about 200 metres — makes up roughly 90% of the ocean's volume and most of its seafloor. Estimates of how many species live there range from a few hundred thousand to more than 10 million. New species are described every year, and genetic tools keep revealing "cryptic" species that were previously lumped together. The vast majority remain unnamed.

Where it starts
Approx. 200 m below the surface
Share of ocean volume
Approx. 90%
How well known?
Most species remain undescribed

Counts are estimates. The World Register of Marine Species (WoRMS) and the Ocean Biodiversity Information System (OBIS) record new deep-sea species as they are described.

02 · Meet the residents

Five animals of the deep

From a patient ambusher with its own lantern to a squid the size of a bus, the deep sea rewards imagination.

🎣
AnglerfishAmbusher

Deep-sea anglerfish

Various families in the suborder Ceratioidei

Size: Females of most species are 2–30 cm; males are much smaller.

Habitat: Bathypelagic zone, roughly 200–2,000 m.

Fact: The female carries a lure — a modified fin ray tipped with a glowing bulb — that she dangles in front of her mouth. In many species, males are tiny and permanently attach to the female.

🦑
Giant squidLargest invertebrate eyes

Giant squid

Architeuthis dux

Size: Up to about 13 m total length, with the mantle around 2 m.

Habitat: Continental slopes and open water, roughly 300–1,000 m.

Fact: Its eyes can reach 27 cm across — the largest of any known animal. They may help it detect the faint glow of sperm whales hunting in the dark.

🦇
Vampire squidNot a true squid

Vampire squid

Vampyroteuthis infernalis

Size: Mantle around 13 cm; total length up to about 30 cm.

Habitat: Oxygen-minimum zones, roughly 600–1,200 m, in warm and temperate oceans.

Fact: Despite its name it is neither a squid nor a vampire — it is the only living member of its own order, and it eats "marine snow" (organic debris falling from above).

👽
Gulper eelHinged jaw

Gulper eel

Eurypharynx pelecanoides

Size: Usually up to about 75 cm, with a whip-like tail.

Habitat: Mesopelagic and bathypelagic waters, roughly 500–3,000 m.

Fact: Its enormous, loosely hinged mouth works like a pelican's pouch — it can engulf prey larger than its own body, then snap shut.

🐙
Dumbo octopusDeepest octopus

Dumbo octopus

Genus Grimpoteuthis

Size: Small — typically 20–30 cm total length.

Habitat: Deep seafloor, roughly 100–7,000 m.

Fact: The "ears" are actually fins that flap to help it swim just above the seafloor. It has no ink sac and uses its webbed arms to hover.

"Deep sea" is defined by depth, not by a single group of animals. It contains fish, cephalopods, crustaceans, cnidarians, and many phyla with no shallow-water relatives at all.

03 · The water column

How the deep sea is layered

Scientists divide the ocean by depth. Each layer has its own light level, pressure, and community of animals.

Epipelagic Sunlight zone — the only layer where photosynthesis is possible 0–200 m
Mesopelagic Twilight zone — faint blue light; home to lanternfish and hatchetfish 200–1,000 m
Bathypelagic Midnight zone — no sunlight at all; anglerfish, giant squid, gulper eels 1,000–4,000 m
Abyssopelagic Abyss — vast plains of soft sediment; dumbo octopuses, sea cucumbers 4,000–6,000 m
Hadalpelagic Hadal zone — deep-sea trenches; snailfish, amphipods, xenophyophores 6,000–11,000 m

Most of this page's animals live in the mesopelagic and bathypelagic zones. The deepest fish ever filmed, a snailfish, was recorded at about 8,336 m in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench.

A giant squid drifts slowly in dark water, its arms and long tentacles undulating

Each of the squid's arms and the two long feeding tentacles sways on its own phase, so the animal never looks stiff. The body drifts slowly up and down — a calm, drifting rhythm, not a churning sprint.

04 · Extreme adaptations

How life survives the abyss

No sunlight, crushing pressure, near-freezing temperatures, scarce food. Deep-sea animals have evolved extraordinary solutions for each problem.

BIOLUMINESCENCE

Making your own light

Perhaps 76% of deep-sea animals can produce light through a chemical reaction. It is used as a lure, a defence, a warning, and possibly to find a mate. Some animals also host glowing bacteria.

PRESSURE

Living under crushing weight

At 4,000 m, pressure is about 400 times what we feel at the surface. Deep-sea fish lack air-filled swim bladders that would collapse; their cells use pressure-tolerant proteins and membranes.

SLOW METABOLISM

A very slow life

Food is rare and unpredictable. Many deep-sea animals burn energy slowly, grow slowly, and can survive long fasts between meals. Some deep-sea fish may live for decades.

GIGANTISM

Why are some deep-sea animals so big?

Deep-sea gigantism is a recurring pattern — giant squid, colossal squid, giant isopods, giant tubeworms. Cold temperatures and slow growth may allow some species to reach much larger sizes than their shallow relatives.

CHEMOSYNTHESIS

Life without sunlight

At hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, bacteria turn hydrogen sulfide and other chemicals into food — the base of entire food webs that need no sunlight. Tubeworms, vent crabs, and yeti crabs depend on this.

TRAPS & TRICKS

Catching what little comes

Enormous mouths (gulper eel), hinged jaws (dragonfish), long sticky tentacles (jellyfish), and needle-like teeth (viperfish) all help deep-sea predators grab scarce prey on the first attempt.

05 · Food webs

What deep-sea animals eat

Without sunlight, deep-sea food webs start with particles falling from above — or with chemistry at the seafloor.

MARINE SNOW

The deep-sea rain

Marine snow is a continuous drift of dead plankton, faecal pellets, and other organic debris from the sunlit zone. It is the main food source for a huge number of deep-sea animals.

PREDATORS

Armed ambush

Many deep-sea fish are ambush predators. They wait, motionless, for prey to drift within reach — relying on lures, huge mouths, or expandable stomachs to make the most of every meal.

SCAVENGERS

A meal the size of a whale

A dead whale can support an entire ecosystem for decades, from sharks and hagfish to bone-eating worms. Whale falls are one of the great events of the deep sea.

CHEMOSYNTHETIC WEBS

Food from chemistry

At hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, chemosynthetic bacteria use hydrogen sulfide instead of sunlight. Vent animals — tubeworms, crabs, clams, shrimp — depend on those bacteria, often living inside their own bodies.

06 · Life cycle

How deep-sea animals reproduce

Finding a mate in the dark, featureless open ocean is not easy. Some deep-sea animals have evolved extraordinary solutions.

PARASITIC MALES

Fused for life

In many anglerfish species, the male is a tiny dwarf that swims to a female, bites into her body, and fuses — his tissues merge with hers, and he lives the rest of his life as a sperm source attached to her side.

LONG BROODING

Patient parents

Some deep-sea octopuses brood their eggs for years — Graneledone boreopacifica was recorded brooding for 53 months. In cold, stable water, slow development is normal.

LOW FECUNDITY

Few but large young

Many deep-sea animals produce relatively few, well-provisioned offspring rather than millions of tiny larvae. This suits a food-poor world but makes recovery from disturbance slow.

LARGE EGGS

Starting big

Deep-sea fish often have larger eggs than their shallow relatives. Some hatch as advanced juveniles — bypassing a fragile larval stage entirely.

The sequence above covers patterns, not rules. Reproductive strategies vary widely among deep-sea animals, and many remain unstudied.

07 · Did you know?

Six facts to take to the surface

The deep sea is stranger than any fiction — and much of it has never been seen.

FACT · 01

The deepest fish

A juvenile snailfish (Pseudoliparis) was filmed in 2022 at about 8,336 m in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench. At that depth, the pressure is over 800 times what we feel at the surface.

FACT · 02

The largest eyes ever

Giant squid eyes reach roughly 27 cm across — larger than a dinner plate. They may help the animal spot the faint bioluminescent wake of a diving sperm whale.

FACT · 03

The vampire's diet

Vampire squid eat marine snow — dead plankton and organic scraps drifting down from above. They are the ocean's patient cleaners, not predators of the living.

FACT · 04

A transparent head

The barreleye fish Macropinna microstoma has a transparent, dome-like head. The green lenses that are its eyes can rotate within the dome, tracking prey above it.

FACT · 05

Light is common

An estimated 76% of deep-sea animals can produce light. The open deep sea is not a black void — it sparkles with flashes, glows, and living lures.

FACT · 06

The hadal zone is bigger than it looks

Trenches deeper than 6,000 m cover less than 1% of the seafloor, but they include the deepest places on Earth — the Mariana Trench reaches about 10,935 m.

08 · A changing deep

Deep-sea life under pressure

The deep sea may be remote, but it is not untouched. Fishing, mining, and warming all reach it.

The main pressures on deep-sea life are bottom trawling (which scrapes the seafloor for fish and shrimp), deep-sea mining for metals used in batteries, shipwrecks and plastic pollution that sink to the abyss, and the slow but global effects of climate change. Because deep-sea animals grow slowly and reproduce slowly, they recover extremely slowly from damage. The deep sea is also one of the least regulated environments on Earth — a challenge for conservation.

Main threat
Bottom trawling
Emerging threat
Deep-sea mining
Why recovery is slow
Slow growth, low reproduction, cold water

Conservation status is known for only a small fraction of deep-sea species. The IUCN Red List is actively expanding its coverage of deep-sea life.
