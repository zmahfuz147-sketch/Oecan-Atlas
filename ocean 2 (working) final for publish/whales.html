The World of Whales & Dolphins

They breathe air, nurse their young, and sing across entire ocean basins. Whales and dolphins are mammals that returned to the sea—and in doing so became some of the largest, loudest, and most intelligent animals on Earth.

01 · The numbers

How many cetaceans exist?

Cetacea is the order that includes whales, dolphins, and porpoises. The line between “whale” and “dolphin” is partly about size, partly about family.

🐋

Living cetaceans · Cetacea

Approx. 90+

Described living species of whales, dolphins, and porpoises

Cetaceans are divided into two suborders. Mysticeti—the baleen whales—filter plankton and small fish using baleen plates; it includes blue, humpback, and right whales. Odontoceti—the toothed whales—hunt fish, squid, and other prey using teeth and, often, echolocation; it includes sperm whales, orcas, dolphins, and porpoises. Orcas and pilot whales are technically dolphins.

Baleen whales
Mysticeti, incl. blue and humpback whales
Toothed whales

Counts are approximate and depend on the taxonomic source. The Society for Marine Mammalogy and the IUCN maintain current lists and assessments for cetaceans.

02 · Meet the groups

Five cetaceans worth knowing

From the largest animal that has ever lived to a small, pale dolphin of tropical rivers, cetaceans span an extraordinary range of forms.

🌊
MysticetiLargest animal

Blue whale

Balaenoptera musculus

Size: Up to roughly 30 m long and about 150 tonnes—the largest animal known to have lived.

Habitat: Open ocean worldwide, feeding in cold, productive waters and breeding in warmer seas.

Fact: Its call can travel hundreds of kilometres underwater, and its heart alone is about the size of a small car.

🎶
MysticetiSinger

Humpback whale

Megaptera novaeangliae

Size: Commonly 12–16 m; females are slightly larger than males.

Habitat: All oceans; migrates between cold feeding grounds and warm breeding waters.

Fact: Males sing long, structured songs on breeding grounds; populations also use bubble-net feeding to corral fish.

🐬
OdontocetiTop predator

Orca

Orcinus orca

Size: Males up to roughly 9–10 m, females around 7–8 m.

Habitat: All oceans, from polar seas to tropical waters; often coastal or continental-shelf regions.

Fact: Despite the “killer whale” name, the orca is the largest member of the dolphin family, with complex, culturally transmitted hunting traditions.

🔊
OdontocetiEcholocator

Bottlenose dolphin

Tursiops truncatus

Size: Typically about 2–4 m, depending on region and sex.

Habitat: Coastal waters, bays, estuaries, and open ocean in warm and temperate seas.

Fact: It uses echolocation to find prey and navigate, and some populations use sponges as tools to protect their snouts while foraging.

🕳️
OdontocetiChampion diver

Cuvier’s beaked whale

Ziphius cavirostris

Size: Around 5–7 m, with females slightly larger than males.

Habitat: Deep offshore waters in all oceans; rarely seen at the surface.

Fact: It holds the record for the deepest and longest dives by a mammal—nearly 3,000 m down for over two hours.

“Whale” and “dolphin” are common names, not strict taxonomic ranks. Orcas and pilot whales, for example, are dolphins, and several “whales” are more closely related to dolphins than to baleen whales.

03 · The deep sea

Whales and dolphins of the deep

Some cetaceans spend most of their lives below the reach of light. They are among the hardest marine mammals to study—and among the most extraordinary.

DEEP · 01

Cuvier’s beaked whale

Ziphius cavirostris

  • Depth: regularly below 1,000 m; record dive approx. 2,992 m
  • Size: approx. 5–7 m
  • Range: all oceans, offshore

A deep-diving odontocete that hunts squid in near-total darkness. Its record dive—nearly 3 km down for over two hours—is the deepest and longest documented for any mammal.

DEEP · 02

Sperm whale

Physeter macrocephalus

  • Depth: routinely 400–1,000 m; deeper dives recorded
  • Size: males up to approx. 18–20 m; females smaller
  • Range: deep waters of all oceans

The largest toothed predator on Earth, with the largest brain of any animal. It hunts deep-sea squid and produces powerful clicks used for both echolocation and communication.

DEEP · 03

Long-finned pilot whale

Globicephala melas

  • Depth: typically a few hundred metres; dives to approx. 1,000 m recorded
  • Size: approx. 5–7 m
  • Range: North Atlantic and Southern Ocean

A large dolphin that lives in tight, matrilineal pods. Its mass strandings have made it a focus of research on cetacean social bonds and navigation.

DEEP · 04

Narwhal

Monodon monoceros

  • Depth: routinely to approx. 1,500 m, often under sea ice
  • Size: approx. 4–5.5 m, excluding the tusk
  • Range: Arctic waters of Canada, Greenland, and Russia

The tusk of a male narwhal is an elongated, spiralled tooth—not a horn—packed with nerve endings. Narwhals dive deep beneath Arctic ice to feed on squid, fish, and shrimp.

Depth records differ between studies and are hard to verify for elusive species. Values above are approximate reference ranges, not limits for every individual.

05 · Regional focus

Whales and dolphins of Asian and European waters

The Indo-Pacific and the Northeast Atlantic host very different cetacean communities, from pink river dolphins to cold-water rorquals.

ASIA · 01

Indo-Pacific humpback dolphin

Sousa chinensis

  • Depth: shallow coastal waters, usually under 30 m
  • Size: approx. 2–2.8 m
  • Range: eastern Indian Ocean and western Pacific, including Hong Kong and Taiwan

Adults often turn pinkish-white as they age, especially in the Taiwanese population, where they are known as “pink dolphins.” Coastal development and fishing gear are major threats.

ASIA · 02

Yangtze finless porpoise

Neophocaena asiaeorientalis

  • Habitat: freshwater rivers and lakes in China
  • Size: approx. 1.5–2 m
  • Status: critically endangered

A small, toothless-looking porpoise (it has teeth, but they are small and peg-like) adapted to turbid freshwater. Fewer than about 1,200 individuals remain in the Yangtze.

EUROPE · 01

Minke whale

Balaenoptera acutorostrata

  • Depth: mostly surface to a few hundred metres
  • Size: approx. 7–10 m
  • Range: North Atlantic, North Pacific, and Southern Ocean

The smallest of the common rorquals in the North Atlantic. It feeds on small fish and krill and is one of the few baleen whales still legally hunted by some countries.

EUROPE · 02

Short-beaked common dolphin

Delphinus delphis

  • Depth: mostly surface to roughly 200 m
  • Size: approx. 1.7–2.4 m
  • Range: Northeast Atlantic, Mediterranean, and other temperate seas

A fast, acrobatic dolphin with a distinctive hourglass pattern on its flanks. Large groups often feed cooperatively on schooling fish such as sardines and anchovies.

EUROPE · 03

Harbour porpoise

Phocoena phocoena

  • Depth: mostly shallow coastal and shelf waters
  • Size: approx. 1.5–2 m
  • Range: North Atlantic and North Pacific coasts

The smallest and most numerous cetacean in many European coastal waters. Unlike dolphins, porpoises have blunt, spade-shaped teeth and lack a prominent beak.

Regional depth and size ranges are compiled from species databases, IUCN assessments, and survey records. Local populations may fall outside these ranges.

06 · Deep time

Fossil whales and the return to the sea

Cetaceans evolved from four-legged land mammals. The fossil record captures the stages of that extraordinary transition.

FOSSIL · 01

Pakicetus

An early whale from Pakistan

  • Time: approx. 50 million years ago, Eocene
  • Size: roughly wolf-sized
  • Known from: skull and partial skeleton

Fossils only. Pakicetus was a four-legged, hoofed mammal that lived near water. Its skull already shows ear features linked to underwater hearing, making it a key transitional fossil.

FOSSIL · 02

Basilosaurus

A fully aquatic early whale

  • Time: approx. 41–34 million years ago, Eocene
  • Size: up to approx. 18 m
  • Known from: skeletons in North Africa and North America

Fossils only. Long and serpentine, Basilosaurus was fully aquatic but still had small hind limbs. It swam using undulations of its body, more like an eel than a modern whale.

FOSSIL · 03

Livyatan melvillei

A giant fossil sperm whale

  • Time: approx. 9–10 million years ago, Miocene
  • Size: estimated 13–17 m
  • Known from: skull and teeth in Peru

Fossils only. Named after Herman Melville, this sperm-whale relative had enormous teeth in both jaws—unlike modern sperm whales, which have teeth only in the lower jaw. It likely hunted large prey.

The cetacean fossil record includes dozens of well-documented transitional forms. Reconstructions of soft anatomy and behaviour remain inferential.

07 · Life cycle

How whales and dolphins reproduce

All cetaceans give birth to live young and nurse them with rich milk. Many species also raise calves in extended family groups.

01 · MATING

Competition and courtship

Mating systems range from scramble competition to male–male combat and complex songs. In humpbacks, males sing long, structured songs on breeding grounds; in some dolphin species, males form alliances that cooperate in courtship.

02 · GESTATION

Months in the womb

Gestation varies widely: about 10–12 months for many dolphins and small whales, and 11–12 months for humpbacks. Sperm whales carry their young for roughly 14–16 months.

03 · BIRTH

Tail-first arrival

Calves are usually born tail-first, so the blowhole emerges last. This reduces the risk of drowning during the birth process. A mother or “auntie” often helps push the newborn to the surface for its first breath.

04 · NURSING

Rich milk

Cetacean milk is very high in fat—sometimes 30–50%—allowing calves to grow quickly. Nursing can last from several months to over a year, depending on the species.

05 · CALF CARE

Alloparenting

In many dolphins and some whales, other females help care for calves. These “aunties” may babysit, escort, or help push a calf to the surface—behaviour that strengthens social bonds.

VARIATION

Not every species is the same

Birth intervals, calf dependency, and group structure vary enormously. Baleen whales tend to have longer migrations and shorter lactation than many toothed whales.

The sequence above covers common patterns. Reproductive timing and calf care differ widely among species and populations.

08 · Food webs

What do whales and dolphins eat?

The two cetacean suborders have radically different feeding strategies—one filters tiny prey, the other hunts with teeth and echolocation.

FILTER FEEDERS

Baleen whales

Blue, humpback, and right whales strain krill and small fish through baleen plates. Some use bubble nets, lunge feeding, or skim feeding to concentrate prey before swallowing.

TOOTHED HUNTERS

Fish and squid

Most toothed whales and dolphins hunt fish, squid, and crustaceans. Orcas also take seals, penguins, and other marine mammals, using cooperative hunting tactics.

DEEP-SEA SPECIALISTS

A diet of squid

Sperm whales and beaked whales dive deep to hunt squid. Sperm whales can consume enormous quantities of squid and are thought to play a major role in deep-ocean food webs.

COOPERATIVE FEEDING

Teamwork

Humpback whales use bubble nets; some dolphin populations herd fish into tight balls; orcas in some regions intentionally strand themselves on beaches to catch seals. Feeding traditions can be passed down culturally.

09 · Adaptations

Senses, song, and culture

Cetaceans navigate, hunt, and communicate in a world of sound. Many species also show striking evidence of social learning.

ECHOLOCATION

Seeing with sound

Toothed whales produce clicks and listen for echoes to map their surroundings. In sperm whales, the clicks are the loudest sounds produced by any animal.

SONG

Humpback songs

Male humpback whales sing long, structured songs on breeding grounds. Songs evolve over time and spread between populations—a form of cultural transmission.

SIGNATURE WHISTLES

Individual names

Bottlenose dolphins use individually distinctive “signature whistles” that function like names. Dolphins can learn and use the signature whistles of close associates.

CULTURE

Traditions passed down

Orca populations have distinct hunting traditions—from seal-hunting in the North Pacific to fish-eating in the Northeast Atlantic—that are passed down socially rather than genetically.

TOOL USE

Sponging dolphins

Some bottlenose dolphins in Shark Bay, Australia, carry sponges on their snouts while foraging. The behaviour is transmitted from mother to calf and is considered a form of tool use.

MIGRATION

Ocean-spanning journeys

Many baleen whales migrate thousands of kilometres between feeding and breeding grounds. Satellite tagging has revealed record-breaking individual journeys across entire ocean basins.

10 · Did you know?

Five facts about whales and dolphins

They are mammals, they are loud, and they have been shaped by millions of years of life in the sea.

FACT · 01

They are mammals

Whales and dolphins breathe air, give birth to live young, nurse them with milk, and have (vestigial) hair. Their closest living land relatives are hippos.

FACT · 02

A heart the size of a car

A blue whale’s heart is roughly the size of a small car and weighs about 180 kg. Its heartbeat can be heard from over 3 km away with specialised equipment.

FACT · 03

Record-breaking dives

Cuvier’s beaked whales have been recorded diving to nearly 3,000 m and staying underwater for over two hours—deeper and longer than any other mammal.

FACT · 04

Loudest animal sounds

Sperm whale clicks can exceed 230 decibels—among the loudest sounds produced by any animal. They are used both for echolocation and communication.

FACT · 05

Half a brain at a time

Many dolphins and some whales sleep with one brain hemisphere at a time, keeping the other awake to surface for air and watch for predators.

11 · A changing ocean

Cetaceans under pressure

Ship strikes, entanglement, noise, pollution, and climate change all affect cetaceans. Many populations are still recovering from historic whaling.

Commercial whaling in the 19th and 20th centuries reduced many large whale populations by more than 90%. Some—such as the North Atlantic right whale—remain critically endangered. Today, the main pressures are entanglement in fishing gear, ship strikes, underwater noise, chemical and plastic pollution, and the shifting distribution of prey driven by climate change. Conservation tools include marine protected areas, speed limits for ships, modified fishing gear, and international agreements such as the IWC ban on commercial whaling.

Historic threat
Commercial whaling
Current threats
Entanglement, ship strikes, noise, pollution
Key tools
Protected areas, gear modification, ship speed limits

Conservation status differs widely between species and populations. Always check the most recent IUCN Red List assessment for a particular species and stock.
